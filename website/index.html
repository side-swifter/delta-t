Cooling that knows the dew point.

A test rack cooled by fans and a liquid loop, and a controller that decides every second how much heat each one carries. It picks the split that uses the least power, keeping chips under 70 °C and the coolant above the dew point.

Try the dew point math → How it works

Heat

Four heaters replay a real server.

Cartridge heaters in machined aluminum blocks follow a 45-minute load recording from our own server, watt for watt. Every run sees the same work, so the only thing that changes is how it gets cooled.

The lower two nodes are air-cooled. The upper two carry cold plates, like a hall halfway through a liquid retrofit.

Air

Fans are cheap to fit and expensive to run.

Fan power rises with roughly the cube of fan speed. Slow the fans by a fifth and they draw about half the power. Every watt the liquid loop can safely take lets the fans slow down.

Pfan ∝ N³    0.8³ = 0.51

Liquid

Colder coolant works better, until water forms on the board.

A Peltier stage chills the coolant below room temperature. Push it below the dew point of the room air and water beads on the cold plates, right above live electronics.

The coldest safe coolant is the dew point plus 2 °C, and it moves with the weather.

Control

Every second, it picks the cheapest safe split.

  1. LogChip, coolant, air, humidity, flow and power, once a second.
  2. ModelEach node's heat flow, fitted to the logs and checked on unseen data.
  3. ForecastServer load 30 to 120 seconds ahead.
  4. SearchFan and pump settings for the lowest power that keeps every limit.

Runs offline on a Raspberry Pi 5 and an ESP32-S3. No cloud and no reinforcement learning.

Questions

The ones judges ask

Isn't this just a thermostat?

A thermostat holds one temperature. Delta-T chooses between two cooling systems with different costs, under two limits that move: chip temperature and the dew point.

How much energy does it save?

We don't know yet, and we won't guess. It gets measured on the rig, for the same workload at equal safety, against a properly tuned standard controller.

Why not reinforcement learning?

It would be harder to trust. A fitted physical model and a small search over fan and pump settings is fast, explainable and easy to check against the limits.

What if the controller fails?

Thermal fuses and a leak sensor on a hardware relay cut power without asking the software. The controller is never the last line of defense.

Who is it for?

Operators of older air-cooled data centers adding liquid cooling for AI hardware, who have to run both systems in the same room.

Where does the load come from?

A 45-minute recording from our own server, replayed through the heaters watt for watt, so every test run does the same work.

The difference is the temperature. We're measuring it.
